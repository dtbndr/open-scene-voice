## The City That Would Not Fall
For a thousand years, Constantinople had been the last great light of the Roman world. Its walls had turned back Persians, Arabs, Avars, and Bulgars. Empires rose and collapsed around it, yet the city endured. In the spring of 1453, it faced its final siege.

## A Shrinking Empire
By then, the Byzantine Empire was little more than a memory with a capital attached. Centuries of war, plague, and betrayal had stripped away almost everything. Where once it had ruled from Spain to Syria, it now held the city, a few islands, and a scrap of the Greek mainland.

The emperor, Constantine the Eleventh, ruled over perhaps fifty thousand people inside the walls. Outside them, the Ottoman Empire numbered in the millions.

## The Young Sultan
Mehmed the Second came to the throne at nineteen. Many in Europe dismissed him as an inexperienced boy. They were badly wrong. Mehmed studied history, languages, and warfare with an obsession that bordered on hunger. He had one goal above all others. He would take the city that generations of his ancestors had failed to conquer.

## Walls Within Walls
The Theodosian Walls were a marvel of ancient engineering. Three layers of defense stretched for miles, from the Golden Horn to the Sea of Marmara. A wide moat lay in front. Then an outer wall, then an inner one, towering above it. For over a thousand years, no army had breached them.

Mehmed knew that ordinary siege methods would never be enough. He needed something new.

## The Great Cannon
The answer was a Hungarian engineer named Orban, who had first offered his services to the Byzantines. They could not afford him. Mehmed could. Orban cast a bronze cannon so enormous that it needed dozens of oxen and hundreds of men to move it. Its stone shot weighed more than a thousand pounds.

When it fired, the sound could be heard for miles. When it hit, the ancient walls began to crack.

## Ships Over the Hills
The defenders had also stretched an iron chain across the mouth of the Golden Horn, sealing the harbor from the Bosphorus. Mehmed's fleet could not break through. So he did something that seemed impossible. He ordered his men to build a road of greased logs over the hills, and in a single night they dragged dozens of ships overland into the harbor.

The defenders woke to find an enemy fleet inside their last line of defense.

## The Final Night
On the evening of May twenty-eighth, the last Christian service was held in the Hagia Sophia. Emperor and commoner knelt together, Orthodox and Catholic, in a rare moment of unity. Then the bells fell silent.

Before dawn, the assault began. Wave after wave hit the walls. The Janissaries, the sultan's elite troops, pushed through a small gate that had been left unlocked. Giustiniani, the great defender, was wounded and carried away. The line broke. Constantine cast off his imperial robes and charged into the fighting. He was never seen again.

## The World After
By afternoon, the Ottoman flag flew over the city. The Hagia Sophia became a mosque. Constantinople became Istanbul, the new heart of a vast empire that would last almost five hundred years.

Scholars fled west, carrying ancient Greek manuscripts with them, and historians still debate how much that migration helped ignite the Renaissance. What is certain is this. The medieval world ended that day, and the modern one began.
